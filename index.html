Summer for the teachers, pandemic for the students

We digitized ten pages from a Vietnamese school literary magazine printed as schools reopened after the 2020 COVID-19 closures, and asked whose writing the year actually reached.

01

Question and audience

Research question. In the May 2020 issue of Văn học và Tuổi trẻ, what vocabulary do teacher-written creative pieces and student essays use to represent the school year, and how much does each group's writing register the events of early 2020?

  1. Themes: what does each group write about most?
  2. Time: does each group look back, or at the present?
  3. School: what does "going to school" look like in each?

Audience. Literature teachers and secondary students who use this magazine as model writing. They need to know whether these model texts reflect students' real lives, and whose voice students are being taught to write in.

Why this issue. Schools closed in February 2020; most students returned in May. This rare double issue sits right at that moment, and we found no digital copy of it online.

AI check: fluent is not the same as faithful

We ran every processed page through DeepAI Chat, one page per message, with the class prompt: "Transcribe this image exactly as written. Keep the line breaks. Do not correct spelling. If you can't read a word, write [???]." We compared it with Tesseract OCR and checked all 107 disagreements against the printed page.

DeepAI was close to perfect on plain columns, but it never wrote [???]. Where it was unsure, it chose a real word that fits, so its errors read as Vietnamese. OCR's errors look like errors.

05

Three decisions that changed the results

1. Joining compound words for Voyant

Problem
Voyant splits Vietnamese by syllable: tin giả becomes tin + giả.
Alternative
Leave the text unjoined, which over-counts tin (29) and học (20).
Trade-off
Joining fixes those counts but scatters dịch into đại_dịch, dịch_bệnh, nghỉ_dịch, mùa_dịch. The pandemic almost vanishes from the word cloud.
What we did
Joined ~250 compounds from a checked list, counted the pandemic as a word group, and kept an unjoined corpus for dịch and mùa.
"dịch" standing alone in student texts23 → 2unjoined → joined

2. Removing the essay prompts from the student corpus

Problem
The p. 94 prompt already says Covid-19, virus corona, nghỉ học, trải nghiệm ý nghĩa, thay đổi tích cực. Those are adult words.
Alternative
Keep the prompts, which inflates the students' pandemic count with language they did not write.
Trade-off
We lose a direct count of how much the prompt shapes the essay, so we compare the prompts with the essays by close reading (Finding 3).

3. Keeping the magazine's print errors

Problem
The magazine prints đối vợi, bất đầu, ap dụng, giẫy giụa, and calls the virus vi khuẩn.
Alternative
Correct them, so Voyant counts với once instead of splitting it.
Trade-off
Faithful text, and a clear AI test: DeepAI silently "fixed" three of the four spellings despite the prompt. Cost: four words counted separately, none in our word groups.

In our own words. This replaces the video. We wrote these explanations in Vietnamese; Claude translated them into English without adding new ideas.

1 · Joining compound words. We joined compound words because single syllables do not show what the writer means or feels. Tin and giả on their own say nothing; only tin giả (fake news) carries the writer's concern. But joining had a cost: dịch fell from 23 to 2, because it was split into đại_dịch, dịch_bệnh, nghỉ_dịch, mùa_dịch and others, so the pandemic almost disappeared from the word cloud. So we did both: we used the joined text for the top terms, added related words together into word groups, and kept an unjoined copy to check dịch and mùa in context.

2 · Removing the essay prompts. We removed the prompts because they were written by adults. If we had left them in, adult words such as Covid-19, nghỉ học, thay đổi tích cực would be counted as the students' words, and our judgement about the students would not be objective. Keeping the prompts aside also showed us something: the students answer in the shape the prompt sets, so they had to write inside an adult mould.

3 · Keeping the print errors. We kept the print errors because we wanted the text to stay as close to the original as possible. Using AI to transcribe can improve the text and help preserve it, but that "improvement" is also the problem: the AI corrects things on its own and becomes inaccurate. DeepAI changed vợi, bất đầu, ap dụng to the correct spelling even though the prompt told it not to, and we only saw this because we checked every difference against the printed page.

06

Findings

1 · The two groups write about two different Mays

Word groups per 1,000 words

Seven word groups we defined (full word lists in our workbook), counted on the same two files we loaded into Voyant; each group total can be checked by entering its words in Voyant's Document Terms.

Summer and nature make up 85 of every 1,000 teacher words and none of the student words. The pandemic and technology together make up 38 of every 1,000 student words and none of the teacher words.

Even the shared word mùa (season) splits: mùa hạ, mùa hè for the teachers, mùa dịch (the epidemic season) twice for the students.

So what: the two groups feel the summer of 2020 in completely opposite ways. The teachers write about summer in its pure form, which is sometimes less objective than the account of the people who lived through it. The students write about what they actually went through: the epidemic season. Tin_giả leads their words because fake news was a pressing problem at that time.

2 · Students live a different summer from the one adults remember

Nostalgia words are 3.6 times more frequent in the teacher texts (14.5 vs 4.0 per 1,000). The same word nhớ means "remember" for the teachers and "miss" for the students.

"Giờ nhớ lại thấy lòng mình nôn nao khó tả."Teacher, Những chú ve, p. 85: remembering childhood
"tôi hơi hụt hẫng vì nhớ trường, nhớ lớp."Grade 8 student, p. 94: missing school during the closure
"Tôi lập kế hoạch cụ thể từng giờ, từng việc trong ngày."Grade 8 student, p. 95: time as a timetable

So what: the students are living a summer very different from the one the adults remember.

3 · Whose words do the students use?

The teachers' school is classic imagery: học_trò, áo_trắng, bài_giảng của thầy, tan_trường. The students' school has no uniform and no classroom: tự_học (5), bài_tập (3), sách_giáo_khoa (2), video, online lessons.

But the frame is adult. The Grade 8 essay answers its prompt's "meaningful experiences" and "positive change" almost word for word, ending "Cảm ơn kì nghỉ dịch". The Grade 11 essay borrows the war language of the 2020 public campaign: giặc dịch bệnh, chiến sĩ, tuyến đầu, hậu phương, quyết thắng đại dịch (16 war-related words; none in GV).

So what: the students' slogan-like language comes from adults, from the moulds adults set, which the students learn and imitate.

We think all three possible explanations play a part: who is writing, the genre and the prompts the students were given, and how the magazine sorts its texts into sections.

07

Limitations

  • Tiny, uneven sample: five texts, about 2,800 words, one issue. Những chú ve is about 77% of the teacher group, so ve is really one text's word.
  • Writer and genre are tangled: all teacher texts are poems or lyrical prose; both student texts answer set prompts.
  • Selected texts: these are what the editors chose to print. We cannot see what was rejected, or when each text was written.
  • Our categories: we decided which words count as "nostalgia" or "school". Voyant counts both meanings of nhớ as one word.
  • Tools: results depend on our compound list. OCR lost drop capitals; DeepAI corrected and guessed silently; poems lose their line breaks in Voyant.
  • Voyant mirror: voyant-tools.org was down on 24 Oct, so we used the official LINCS mirror (same version, 2.6.22).
08

Conclusion

In this issue, teachers and students feel the summer of 2020 in completely opposite ways. The teachers write a pure summer of cicadas and sunlight. The students write the summer they actually went through: the epidemic season of online lessons, timetables and fake news.

The students' words also come partly from adults: from the prompts they were set and from the slogans they learned and imitated. So when teachers evaluate students' writing, they should put themselves in the position of the person who is actually living the experience.
